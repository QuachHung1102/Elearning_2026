# Product brief and content plan

- The CodingBuddy teaches students aged 10–15.
- Intended direction: teach coding and technology through meaningful projects that can help people or address a concrete problem.
- The coursework is limited. Do not suggest that visitors can learn anything they choose from a large catalog.
- A Vietnamese/English switch is required.
- Hải Nam plans to record 10+ teaching videos himself, focused on building real projects. These are planned, not completed.
- There may also be basic coding materials. The user currently has little other project-specific material.
- Earlier résumé statistics were not approved for publication. No new testimonials or claimed outcomes should be invented.
- Preparation and demo review precede implementation; the user must approve implementation explicitly.

## Product proposition

A small, guided place to learn coding by making a useful project, taught by Hải Nam. A learner should understand what they will build, what knowledge they need, how the lessons connect, and how to start.

Students are the primary audience. Parents may need practical information about prerequisites, format, contact, and fees. The application deadline is a delivery constraint, not the audience of the website.

The community-education ambition belongs in the teaching approach as well as the project story. Describe intended support as an intention until activities and financial commitments are confirmed. Do not turn a broad mission into claims of measured results.

## Proposed content strategy

Build the initial website around one complete teaching sequence. A small curriculum is legible when it has a destination and a sensible sequence. Do not pad it with empty subjects, artificial course categories, or sample offerings presented as available.

Possible flagship projects; choose based on what Hải Nam can teach and debug confidently:

| Candidate | A specific person it might help | A beginner-sized version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Study-resource directory | Younger students who struggle to locate useful materials | A short collection of resources with subject labels and simple search |
| Classroom book-sharing tracker | Classmates looking to borrow or share a book | A list of titles with available/borrowed status, using local sample data |
| Accessible digital-skills guide | Someone learning basic computer tasks | A few clear pages explaining one task, with readable steps and tested navigation |

These are curriculum suggestions, not existing CodingBuddy projects. Do not imply they have beneficiaries or demonstrated impact. Keep the first version small; no student accounts, personal-data collection, AI integrations, or complex deployment are necessary inside the teaching project.

## Ten-video teaching outline

The sequence is adaptable to the chosen language and project. Proposed durations are 5–8 minutes, with shorter clips where the topic needs less time.

1. Meet the problem and see the finished project. Explain who it is for and what the first version will do.
2. Set up the tools and run the smallest working example.
3. Learn the first essential concept through one change to the project.
4. Represent the project's information with the simplest suitable structure.
5. Handle one input or user action.
6. Add the central feature: finding, counting, displaying, or updating information.
7. Break it deliberately and debug the failure on screen.
8. Make it easier to use; consider labels, reading order, and accessibility where applicable.
9. Try the project with someone, or conduct an explicitly labeled self-test if no tester is available. Explain limitations honestly.
10. Review the complete build, improve one part, and give students one achievable extension.

Each lesson needs a concrete outcome, the relevant starter state, an exercise, and a clear next lesson. Teach the underlying idea rather than merely narrating keystrokes. A small game or playful exercise can teach a foundation; every individual lesson does not need a social-impact claim.

## Material Hải Nam can create now

### Highest value

- One working flagship project and 3–5 real screenshots of its development. Show the rough version and a useful revision.
- A 45–60 second introduction, recorded simply: who you are, whom you teach, and what learners will make. A face recording is optional; voice plus screen is enough.
- A one-page course outline: prerequisites, tools, lesson sequence, likely project outcome, and current availability.
- Starter code and a completed example, clearly distinguished. Include short run instructions.
- A concise written companion to each finished video and corrected captions. These help students revisit code and learn with low bandwidth.
- Three practice exercises at different levels: change something, explain something, and make an extension.

### Useful if time remains

- A real debugging note explaining a mistake you made and how you found it.
- A short project reflection: who might use this, what it currently does, and what remains limited.
- A small, genuine usability check with one or two willing peers. Record what changed; do not manufacture praise or turn this into a testimonial collection campaign.

### Avoid producing just to fill the website

Stock classroom scenes, generated student portraits, invented reviews, staged impact metrics, generic topic articles, a decorative AI chatbot, and a large set of empty course tiles.

## Practical one-week cut

Aim for the full series only if recording and editing quality remain manageable. A defensible minimum is one working project, a complete course outline, an introduction, and 2–3 finished lessons with exercises and downloads. Mark remaining lessons as in preparation. The learner must see availability before registering or paying.

Content creation and visual exploration can proceed in parallel. Once a representative lesson and project screenshot exist, use them in the next design proof. Final recorded videos can replace clearly identified preview assets later.

## Bilingual behavior to decide before implementation

- Translate navigation, controls, course summaries, errors, and lesson descriptions deliberately.
- Confirm the language of each recording. An English interface does not imply an English audio track.
- Explain any unavailable translation; do not silently swap languages inside a lesson.
- Switching languages should preserve the current page and learning position.
- Test fonts with Vietnamese diacritics and full Vietnamese sentences before selecting the final type system.

## Remaining decisions

The actual first project and programming language; which lessons will be ready by launch; recording language; free/paid access; any confirmed instructor or institutional relationship; available identity assets; the precise donation commitment. None should be invented to finish a layout.
